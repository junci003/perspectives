# Cases Reference

Ten cases, each labeled with the translation it won on. Use them as evidence when advising on a design decision — not as style references.

## T1 — Form

### 1. Takayuki Takeya, *Shin Godzilla* (2016)
To achieve a "complete organism" horror, Takeya replicated the surface of a bitter melon in silicone, shrank it, and pressed it onto the sculpt. For the dorsal ridge he wanted exposed bone, so he assembled fox and weasel bones together; the tail used snake and fish bones.

**Won on:** surface form via real-material sampling. Realism comes from asymmetry that cannot be drawn from imagination.

**Transferable:** surface detail should be *sampled*, never *designed*. Find a real object — fruit skin, bark, sandpaper, fabric, bone, rusted iron — mold or scan it, scale it, apply locally.

### 2. Takayuki Takeya, S.I.C. series
Method stated plainly: build on a biological skeleton first, then layer muscle and detail. That is why his Kamen Riders read as creatures — not because texture was added, but because the structure underneath was correct.

**Won on:** mass form, skeleton-first.

**Transferable:** when a figure feels "pasted" rather than built, the underlying block structure is missing. Most designers do it backwards — smear the surface, then try to retrofit structure, which never closes the gap.

### 3. 末那工作室, *鬼神志* (Sun Wukong)
The original concept art had a grey-white top. The team compared versions repeatedly and changed it to a dark top because contrast held up better under display lighting.

**Won on:** T1 color decision. Cheapest high-impact call in the whole project.

**Transferable:** test the main color under the actual display light at thumbnail size before committing. A 10-minute comparison prevents a 2-week rework.

### 4. Kumamon (silhouette benchmark)
Round head, short limbs, fixed cheek positions. Identifiable in pure black at distance.

**Won on:** silhouette economy — no stray contour lines.

**Transferable:** use it as the reference standard for "how few elements can carry recognition".

## T2 — Engineering

### 5. Good Smile Company, Nendoroid
Head at roughly one third of total height; three face plates as standard; uniform ball-and-socket joints at neck, shoulders, legs; face plates seat on a spherical peg hidden inside the hair.

**Won on:** turning "swappable" into a **standardized part system**. The same joint spec is reused across characters, so accessories from different releases interchange.

**Transferable:** if planning a series, define the joint spec once and hold it. Compatibility across releases compounds into a product line.

### 6. Bing Dwen Dwen (Beijing 2022)
The translucent ice shell reads as one piece; in production the shell and the panda body are molded separately and sleeved together, giving near-uniform wall thickness.

**Won on:** using part splitting to create a material effect.

**Transferable:** when a material effect seems impossible, check whether splitting solves it before changing material.

### 7. Line Friends, Brown
Ears molded separately and assembled because a one-piece undercut cannot be ejected.

**Won on:** accepting the engineering constraint and turning it into design language — the ear seam is where an ear would naturally have one.

**Transferable:** a mandatory seam should be placed where the object would plausibly have a seam anyway.

### 8. Infinity Studio, 1/3 Cammy (Street Fighter 6)
65 cm tall; internal alloy skeleton with 31 articulated joints; full platinum-silicone skin hiding every ball joint; articulated glass eyes; hand-rooted hair with wire-cored braids; hand-tailored fabric costume.

**Won on:** making engineering itself the price justification. 31 joints is not a cost, it is a reason to charge.

**Transferable:** at the top of the market, technical capability is the product. Count your engineering features and market them explicitly.

## T0 — Planning

### 9. Good Smile Company planning flow
The planning team (about six people) fixes pose and expression from source material first, writes a 仕様書, draws the design sheet, and sends it for licensor review. Only after spec → unpainted prototype → painted sample approvals does the figure go to the factory for part splitting and tooling. Manufacturing objections (e.g. "can we use a special material to make the eyes glow") are resolved at the spec stage by substitution ("use a paint that reads as glow").

**Won on:** moving all disagreement before the mold cost.

**Transferable:** every "can we technically do X" question must be answered in the spec, not after sampling.

## Cross-Case Patterns

| Pattern | Cases | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Detail is sampled, not designed | 1, 4 | Use real material references |
| Structure precedes surface | 2, 5 | Block out mass and skeleton first |
| Splitting creates properties | 6, 7 | Before changing material, try splitting |
| Engineering as marketing | 8 | Count and name your technical features |
| Standardize to build a line | 5 | One joint spec across the series |
| Resolve disagreement early | 9 | Spec stage, not sample stage |
